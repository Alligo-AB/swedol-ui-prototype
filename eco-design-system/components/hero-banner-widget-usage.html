Hero banner

The Hero banner is a versatile component for campaign features and for the headers of landing pages, on campaign pages and sites and on brand pages. It has many settings for building responsive hero sections.

When to use a hero banner, fluid or regular width, and the do’s and don’ts for images, text, color, buttons and branding.

The hero banner’s purpose

Hero banners are high-impact, visually dominant components that capture attention at once and carry your most important message. Use them strategically, not by default.

For the settings of the component, see the Storyblok tab. For finished banners to learn from, see Reference and Examples.

Fluid vs Regular width

Fluid width

The banner extends edge to edge across the whole viewport, with no side padding. It creates a full-bleed, immersive experience.

Regular width

The banner follows the standard page padding and keeps its spacing to the other elements on the page. It looks more contained and structured.

Choosing between the two is a judgement call, and it depends on several things. The directives below help. As a rule of thumb, use the component consistently across similar page types.

When to use the regular width

Content focused

  • For campaigns, for example on the home page.
  • On campaign landing pages where you do not need to bang the big drum.
  • In page content, when the banner needs to feel like part of the content flow.
  • Limited or mediocre imagery that will not hold up at large sizes: use the regular width.
  • When users are task oriented rather than in exploration mode.
  • Do not go full width just because you can.

When to use the fluid width

High impact moments

  • Home page hero sections where the first impression matters, such as product launches, major announcements or major campaigns.
  • You want to create a sense of scale or grandeur.
  • You are establishing a brand atmosphere before the content.
  • You break away from the structure of the rest of the page for emphasis.

Campaign landing pages that need maximum attention

  • Landing page headers for campaign sites, or promoting a service, or brand landing pages (for example AmPro, Bosch, Makita).
  • Landing pages that promote products.
  • Premium product positioning.
  • Brand awareness campaigns.
  • Campaign microsites.
  • Giving context for what users find further down the page, in a promoting role.

When to consider

Content-heavy landing pages

  • Educational content pages
  • How-to guides or resource centres
  • Article-style landing pages
  • Multi-section content experiences
  • Blog and article
  • Category gateway page or PLP
  • Sub-category page

Consider: Will the hero compete with the content below, or support it? Does the page benefit from immediate visual impact, or should the content come first?

Best practice: Make sure the hero introduces the content and does not dominate it. Use an image that represents the content or the topic.

Service or information pages

  • Delivery information pages
  • Customer service sections
  • About us pages
  • Store locator pages

Consider: Do these functional pages benefit from a hero, or does a simpler header do?

Best practice: Use a simpler hero without heavy campaign elements. Focus on clarity over impact.

When not to use

Content needs to be front and center

  • Product listing pages, where the products are the priority
  • Product detail pages, where the product information is the priority
  • Search results pages
  • Comparison or filter-heavy pages
  • Pages where users want direct access to tools or content

Why: A hero can push the content below the fold. If the content is why users are there, do not make them scroll past a hero first.

Alternative: A minimal page header or breadcrumb navigation.

Example: Product search results: users want to see products, not a banner.

You are just filling space

  • “We need something at the top” without a strategic purpose
  • Generic stock imagery with no message
  • Placeholder content “just because”
  • Copying hero settings without thinking

Why: A bad hero is worse than no hero. It adds visual weight without value and slows the user down.

Alternative: A simple, clean page header that does not pretend to be a campaign.

Example: Do not add a generic “Welcome to Tools” hero with stock imagery. It adds no value.

The page is transactional

  • Checkout pages
  • Account management pages
  • Shopping cart
  • Login and registration pages, unless it is a combined USP section that converts future users. The registration form must stay prominent and clearly visible, because the forms are the primary item.
  • Form-heavy pages

Why: These pages are task focused. A hero distracts from completing the transaction.

Alternative: Minimal headers that build trust and guide the process.

Example: No hero on checkout: users are there to buy, not to be marketed to.

The mobile experience would suffer

  • The hero takes up the whole mobile viewport
  • Critical content is pushed too far down on mobile
  • The hero height causes too much scrolling
  • The message is too complex for a small screen

Why: Mobile users should reach the content quickly. An oversized hero hurts the mobile experience.

Alternative: Use breakpoint visibility to show a simplified hero on mobile, or skip the hero on the mobile breakpoints.

Do’s and Don’ts

Responsive design

Do

  • Adjust the content per breakpoint when needed (use breakpoint visibility).
  • Reduce the text size on smaller breakpoints for a better fit.
  • Test the hero on real devices, not only a resized browser.
  • Consider smaller buttons on mobile.

Don’t

  • Assume the desktop design works on mobile.
  • Use the same long text on every breakpoint. The text must still make sense on a small screen.
  • Ignore how the elements stack on small screens.
  • Forget to check landscape orientation on mobile.

Image usage

Do

  • Think about the focal point of the image when it is a background for the text.
  • Test the image on all breakpoints before publishing.
  • Use wider images for a Fluid banner when keeping the proportions matters.
  • Pick images that support the campaign message and the brand identity.

Don’t

  • Put critical visual elements at the extreme edges of the image.
  • Use low-resolution, pixelated or unoptimised images.
  • Upload images with embedded text that repeats the hero text.
  • Use images with busy backgrounds that compete with the text.
  • Forget how horizontal cropping affects the composition.

Text and content

Do

  • Keep headlines short, punchy and easy to scan (5–8 words at most).
  • Use the pre title for context or category.
  • Write short, action-oriented button text.
  • Test the readability of the text on the real image.
  • Adjust the title size for each breakpoint.
  • Use correct grammar and spelling.

Don’t

  • Write long paragraphs in the preamble (keep it to 1–2 sentences).
  • Use generic button text such as “Click here” or “Learn more” without context.
  • Crowd the hero with too much text.
  • Use conflicting messages in the title and the buttons.
  • Forget to proofread before publishing.

Color and contrast

Do

  • Always use the overlay when the readability of the text is at risk.
  • Test both Primary and Primary inverted text with your image if you are unsure which works best.
  • Adjust the overlay opacity to balance image visibility and text legibility.
  • Choose a news badge color that suits the whole design.

Don’t

  • Use a text color that blends into the background.
  • Set the overlay opacity to 0 if the text becomes unreadable.
  • Rely on the image alone for contrast. Always test with the overlay.
  • Use the green news badge outside Swedol campaigns.
  • Use the red news badge outside Tools sites.

Layout and alignment

Do

  • Use left alignment for text-heavy content.
  • Think about the visual weight of all the elements when you position them.

Don’t

  • Put text over busy areas of the background image. The overlay can help.
  • Crowd the hero with too many elements in one area.
  • Ignore the natural reading flow (top to bottom, left to right).

Campaign elements

Do

  • Use the discount badge for clear, short messages (for example “30% OFF”).
  • Show the campaign price and the old price info together, for transparency.
  • Check that every price is correct before publishing.
  • Use campaign elements consistently across related banners.
  • Remove campaign elements promptly when the promotion ends.

Don’t

  • Use the discount badge for non-promotional content.
  • Show a campaign price without the discount badge.
  • Forget to remove expired campaign prices.
  • Use vague discount information. Always be specific.
  • Overuse campaign elements on non-promotional heroes.

Buttons and links

Do

  • Use Button One for the primary call to action.
  • Consider the chevron to emphasise the primary action.
  • Test every link before publishing.
  • Use relative URLs for internal links (for example category/tools).
  • Use https:// for all external links.
  • Choose a button size that matches the importance of the message.

Don’t

  • Use Button overlay when you have two different actions.
  • Link to broken or outdated pages.
  • Use the same link for both buttons. It confuses users.
  • Forget to give the link a destination.
  • Use oversized buttons on mobile breakpoints by default.

Branding elements

Do

  • Use the Power Deal logo only for real Power Deal campaigns.
  • Turn on the Power Deal border when you use the Power Deal logo.
  • Use the news badge sparingly, for genuinely new content.

Don’t

  • Overuse the news badge. It loses its impact.
  • Use Power Deal elements for campaigns that are not Power Deal.
  • Combine too many branding elements in one hero.

Width settings

Do

  • Use Fluid width for edge-to-edge, high-impact campaign heroes.
  • Think about the content around the banner.

Don’t

  • Use Regular width images with the Fluid setting if you do not want the image to scale.
  • Mix width settings at random across similar page types.

Accessibility

Do

  • Write descriptive button text that makes sense out of context.
  • Always add an image description.

Don’t

  • Use images with embedded critical text as the only source of information.

General best practices

Do

  • Review all settings before publishing.
  • Use the component consistently across similar page types.
  • Keep a record of hero setups that worked, for reference.
  • Update the content regularly to keep it fresh.
  • Coordinate the hero design with the overall page strategy.

Don’t

  • Publish without testing on several devices.
  • Make last-minute changes without a review.
  • Ignore the analytics of the hero (this needs testing procedures).
  • Forget to archive or update seasonal campaign heroes.

Use of the chevron on the hero button

info

This section may move to the Button documentation when the button component is done.

What the chevron does

The chevron (→) is a right-pointing arrow on the button. It adds visual weight and direction to the call to action, and signals forward movement. Because the hero is already prominent, the chevron is mostly cosmetic.

When to use the chevron

Primary action emphasis

  • The button is the main conversion goal of the page.
  • You want maximum attention on the call to action.
  • The action is the main reason the hero exists.
  • It is the most important click on the whole page.

Why: The chevron adds weight and urgency, so the primary action is unmistakable.

Example: “Shop now →” on a Black Friday hero.

Navigational actions

  • The button takes users to another page or section.
  • The action moves forward in a journey (for example “Explore collection”, “View products”).
  • The button leads to a category, a product listing or a dedicated landing page.
  • The action implies progress or discovery.

Why: The chevron reinforces the idea of moving forward or going somewhere.

Example: “Discover new arrivals →” leading to a product category.

High-priority campaigns

  • Major seasonal sales (Swedol day, Black Friday, summer sale, holiday promotions)
  • Limited-time offers where urgency matters
  • Flash sales or time-sensitive campaigns
  • Exclusive product launches

Why: The chevron adds urgency and draws the eye, which suits high-stakes campaigns.

Example: “Claim your discount →” on a 48-hour flash sale.

Clear, confident calls to action

  • The button text is direct and action oriented.
  • The message is confident and assertive.
  • The action has clear, immediate value for the user.
  • There is no doubt about what happens next.

Why: A strong call to action deserves a strong visual treatment. The chevron reinforces confidence.

Example: “Start shopping →”, “Get started →”, “See all deals →”.

Single-button scenarios

  • There is only one button on the hero (the primary button).
  • All attention should go to one primary action.
  • The button overlay is on (the whole hero is clickable).
  • The message is singular and focused.

Why: With no competing actions, the chevron adds emphasis without confusion.

Example: A product launch hero with one clear action: “Pre-order now →”.

When not to use the chevron

Already visually busy designs

  • The hero has many elements (discount badge, price, logo, news badge).
  • The design is complex, with overlays and several text elements.
  • More visual elements would create clutter.
  • Users may experience visual overload.

Why: Sometimes simplicity performs better. The chevron can be “one thing too many”.

Better approach: Simplify the other elements first, or skip the chevron for a cleaner look.

Informational or soft actions

  • The button text is exploratory (for example “Learn more”).
  • The action is educational rather than transactional.
  • Users are browsing, not buying.
  • The call to action is passive or investigative.

Why: The chevron implies urgency and forward momentum, which is wrong for casual exploration.

Better without: “Learn more” without a chevron feels less pushy for educational content.

Subtle or minimal designs

  • The overall design is clean and minimalist.
  • The brand look is understated and refined.
  • The hero uses subtle typography and muted colors.
  • Less is more in the design approach.

Why: The chevron adds visual complexity that may conflict with a minimal design.

Consider: Test both versions. Sometimes a minimal design benefits from one strong visual accent.

Secondary buttons

  • You use Button Two (the secondary action).
  • The button is an alternative or fallback.
  • The action has lower priority than the primary button.
  • You offer a “softer” alternative to the main call to action.

Why: Only the primary action should carry maximum visual weight. Two chevrons compete.

Best practice: Button One with a chevron, Button Two without. Example: “Shop sale →” (with chevron) and “View catalog” (no chevron).

Passive or ambiguous calls to action

  • The button text is vague (“Click here”, “More info”).
  • The outcome of the click is unclear.
  • The action has no obvious value.
  • Users may be confused about what happens next.

Why: A chevron on weak copy does not fix the problem. It highlights it.

Better solution: Improve the button text first, then decide on the chevron.
